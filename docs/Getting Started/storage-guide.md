## Why storage matters

Tea absorbs moisture, odors, and heat from its surroundings. Poor storage dulls flavor, introduces off-tastes, and shortens shelf life. Following a few consistent practices keeps each cup tasting as intended.

## Control humidity

Humidity is the most important storage factor for Japanese tea. Excess moisture accelerates oxidation, encourages mold growth, and degrades delicate flavors.

| Humidity level | Effect on tea | Action |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Below 40% RH | Leaves may become overly brittle but flavor is preserved | Acceptable for storage |
| 40–60% RH | Ideal range that balances leaf integrity and freshness | Maintain this range |
| Above 60% RH | Risk of moisture absorption, mold, and stale or musty flavors | Move tea to a drier location or add a desiccant |

### Tips for managing humidity

1. Store tea in an airtight, opaque container to isolate it from ambient moisture.
2. Avoid opening the container in a humid room. Let the container reach room temperature before opening to prevent condensation on the leaves.
3. If you live in a humid climate, place a food-safe desiccant packet inside the storage container and replace it periodically.
4. Keep tea away from the stove, dishwasher, or sink area where steam raises local humidity.

## Control temperature

Heat speeds up chemical reactions that degrade tea. Store tea in a cool location away from direct heat sources.

| Storage method | Temperature range | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Room temperature | 15–25°C / 59–77°F | Tea you plan to use within a few weeks |
| Refrigerator | 2–5°C / 36–41°F | Unopened packages you want to keep for one to three months |
| Freezer | −18°C / 0°F or below | Long-term storage of sealed, unopened tea |

When removing tea from cold storage, let the sealed container warm to room temperature before opening. This prevents condensation from forming on the cold leaves.

## Protect from light and air

- **Light.** UV and visible light break down chlorophyll and amino acids. Use opaque containers or store tea inside a cupboard.
- **Air.** Oxygen accelerates oxidation and flavor loss. Squeeze excess air from resealable bags, or transfer tea to a container with a tight-fitting lid.

## Storage by tea style

| Style | Container | Location | Shelf life guidance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Steamed green tea | Airtight, opaque canister or foil bag | Cool cupboard or refrigerator | Use within a few weeks of opening for the freshest flavor |
| Roasted tea | Airtight canister | Room temperature, away from heat | More forgiving; use within a few months of opening |
| Powdered tea | Airtight, opaque tin | Refrigerator or cool cupboard | Use within a few weeks of opening; flavor and color fade quickly |

## Signs of improper storage

| Symptom | Likely cause |
| --- | --- |
| Musty or stale smell | Excess humidity or moisture exposure |
| Faded leaf color | Light exposure or prolonged heat |
| Flat, dull flavor | Oxidation from air or heat |
| Clumping (powdered tea) | Moisture absorption |

## Optional: log your storage conditions

Record the container type, storage location, date opened, and any changes you notice in aroma or flavor over time. Comparing notes across batches helps you refine your storage setup.
