## Tea production terms

Understanding how tea is processed helps you choose the right style and brew it well. **Steaming** is the heat treatment applied to freshly picked leaves to stop oxidation and preserve their green color and vegetal flavor. Japanese teas are almost always steamed rather than pan-fired, which is the method more common in Chinese green tea production. The length of steaming — light, medium, or deep — affects the leaf structure and the body of the brewed cup.

**Shading** refers to covering tea plants with nets or screens for several weeks before harvest. Reduced sunlight increases the concentration of L-theanine and chlorophyll in the leaves, producing a sweeter, more umami-rich flavor. Matcha and gyokuro are the best-known shaded teas. The term **tencha** describes the shade-grown leaf after steaming and drying but before it is ground into matcha powder.

## Tasting and flavor terms

When reading tea descriptions in the [tea collection](/docs/tea-collection), you will encounter sensory terms that describe naturally occurring aromas and tastes. **Vegetal** refers to green, plant-like flavors such as fresh grass, steamed spinach, or edamame — these are hallmarks of lightly steamed Japanese green teas. **Umami** describes a savory, brothy depth that lingers on the palate, especially in shaded teas. **Astringency** is the dry, puckering sensation caused by catechins; a moderate level adds structure to the cup, but excessive astringency usually signals that the water was too hot or the steep too long.

**Pyrazines** are aromatic compounds created during the roasting process and are responsible for the toasty, nutty, and caramel-like notes found in roasted teas such as hojicha. The term **finish** refers to the flavors and sensations that remain in your mouth after swallowing. A long, sweet finish is generally considered a sign of high-quality leaf and careful processing.

## Brewing and equipment terms

Several equipment terms appear throughout the [brewing guide](/docs/brewing-guide). A **chasen** is the traditional bamboo whisk used to mix matcha powder into water; its fine tines create the smooth, frothy texture that defines a well-prepared bowl of matcha. A **kyusu** is a side-handled Japanese teapot designed for brewing leaf tea — its built-in strainer keeps leaves in the pot while you pour. **Decanting** means pouring all of the brewed liquid out of the pot or infuser at the end of the steep to stop extraction and prevent bitterness.

The term **brewing baseline** appears frequently in teaCapital documentation and refers to the recommended starting recipe — leaf amount, water volume, water temperature, and steep time — for a given tea. It is meant as a reliable first attempt, not a fixed rule. Adjusting one variable at a time from the baseline is the most effective way to tailor the cup to your personal taste.
