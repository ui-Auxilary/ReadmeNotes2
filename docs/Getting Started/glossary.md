## Tea styles

**Sencha** is the most widely produced Japanese green tea. After harvest, the leaves are briefly steamed to halt oxidation, then rolled into tight, needle-like shapes. Steaming preserves the bright green color and fresh, vegetal flavor that distinguish Japanese green tea from Chinese pan-fired varieties. Sencha ranges from light and sweet to bold and astringent depending on the harvest timing, growing region, and steaming duration.

**Gyokuro** is a shade-grown green tea. The tea bushes are covered for approximately three weeks before harvest, which increases the concentration of L-theanine and chlorophyll in the leaves. The result is a deeply savory, umami-rich cup with a thick, almost brothy body. Gyokuro is typically brewed at a lower temperature (50–60°C) and with a higher leaf-to-water ratio than sencha to bring out its distinctive character.

**Matcha** is produced from shade-grown leaves that are de-stemmed, de-veined, and stone-ground into a fine powder. Because you consume the entire leaf, matcha delivers a more concentrated flavor and nutrient profile than steeped teas. It is prepared by whisking the powder into hot water with a chasen until the surface is smooth and lightly frothy. Matcha grades range from ceremonial (intended for drinking straight) to culinary (used in cooking and blending).

**Hojicha** is a roasted Japanese green tea. The roasting process transforms the leaf's color from green to reddish-brown and replaces the vegetal notes with warm, toasty, and nutty flavors. Roasting also reduces the caffeine content, making hojicha a popular choice for evening drinking or for those who prefer a gentler stimulant effect. It brews well at higher temperatures (85–90°C) and pairs naturally with meals.

## Brewing and preparation terms

**Steep time** refers to the duration that tea leaves remain in contact with hot water. Longer steep times extract more flavor, body, and caffeine, but also increase bitterness and astringency. The [brewing guide](/docs/brewing-guide) provides baseline steep times for each tea style as a starting point.

**Decanting** means pouring all of the brewed liquid out of the teapot or infuser after the steep time ends. Fully decanting prevents the leaves from continuing to extract in residual water, which would make the next infusion bitter or thin. It also ensures that each cup from a multi-steep session has a distinct and balanced flavor.

**Water hardness** describes the concentration of dissolved minerals — primarily calcium and magnesium — in your water, measured in parts per million (ppm). Soft water (50–80 ppm) is ideal for Japanese green tea because it allows the tea's natural sweetness and umami to come through. Hard water (above 150 ppm) can mute delicate flavors and leave a chalky residue. A TDS (total dissolved solids) meter provides a quick reading.

**Chasen** is a hand-carved bamboo whisk used to prepare matcha. The tines of the whisk break up powder clumps and incorporate air into the liquid, creating the smooth, frothy texture that defines a well-made bowl of matcha. After each use, rinse the chasen under warm water and dry it on a whisk holder (kusenaoshi) to preserve the shape of the tines.

## Sourcing and quality terms

**First flush** (also called ichibancha) is the first harvest of the tea season, typically picked in late April or May. The leaves have accumulated amino acids during winter dormancy, which gives first-flush teas their characteristic sweetness, complexity, and vivid green color. Later harvests (second flush, third flush) tend to be more astringent and less nuanced, though they are still suitable for everyday drinking and blending.

**Lot** refers to a defined batch of tea that is harvested, processed, and tracked as a single unit. teaCapital assigns a lot identifier to each arrival so that tasting notes, brewing baselines, and quality observations remain connected to a specific batch. If a quality issue arises, the lot system makes it possible to trace the problem back to its source and prevent it from recurring. See [Japanese tea sourcing](/docs/japanese-tea-sourcing) for more on how lots are managed.

**Umami** is a Japanese word meaning "pleasant savory taste." In tea, umami comes primarily from L-theanine, an amino acid that is abundant in shade-grown varieties like gyokuro and matcha. A tea with strong umami tastes rich, brothy, and full-bodied rather than simply bitter or astringent. Brewing at lower temperatures and with more leaf per cup emphasizes umami over other flavor components.

**Cultivar** refers to a specific variety of the tea plant (Camellia sinensis) that has been selectively bred for particular characteristics such as flavor, aroma, disease resistance, or harvest timing. Common Japanese cultivars include Yabukita (the most widely planted, known for balanced flavor), Saemidori (prized for sweetness and umami), and Okumidori (valued for its mild, approachable character). teaCapital notes the cultivar on each product page when the information is available from the producer.
