## Freshness and seasonality

Japanese tea is an agricultural product with a limited window of peak flavor. First-flush teas, harvested in spring, carry the most vibrant and complex character. teaCapital labels each lot with its harvest season and production date so you can gauge freshness before you buy. Once a sealed pouch is opened, the clock starts — exposure to oxygen begins to dull the bright, vegetal notes that define high-quality sencha and gyokuro within weeks.

Plan your purchases around how quickly you brew. A 50 g pouch lasts roughly ten to twelve cups at the standard 5 g dose, which most daily drinkers finish in two to three weeks. If you buy in larger quantities, split the tea into smaller airtight containers and open one at a time. This simple habit preserves the flavor of the remaining tea far better than repeatedly opening a single large bag.

Seasonal and limited-edition teas appear in the [tea collection](/docs/tea-collection) on a rotating basis. These lots are produced in small volumes and are not restocked once sold out, so check availability early if a particular harvest interests you.

## Water and temperature essentials

Water makes up over 98% of a brewed cup, so its quality has an outsized effect on taste. Hard or heavily mineralized water suppresses the sweetness and umami that Japanese green teas are known for, replacing them with a flat or chalky character. teaCapital recommends filtered or soft water in the 50–80 ppm range. If you are unsure about your tap water, an inexpensive TDS meter gives you a reading in seconds.

Temperature control is equally important. Boiling water extracts catechins and tannins aggressively, producing bitterness that overwhelms the tea's natural sweetness. For steamed green teas, let boiled water cool to 70–80°C before pouring. Roasted teas tolerate higher temperatures — 85–90°C — because the roasting process has already reduced the compounds responsible for astringency. Powdered tea sits in between at 80°C, hot enough to disperse the powder but cool enough to avoid a harsh edge.
